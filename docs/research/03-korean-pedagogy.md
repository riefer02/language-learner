# Korean Pedagogy for English-Speaking Learners

**Research brief for curriculum design · October 2026**

Scope: Korean-specific linguistics and pedagogy for adult L1-English learners, zero to TOPIK 6. Empirical claims are sourced; estimates (notably Hangul timings and coverage percentages) are labelled as design judgements.

---

## 1. Hangul: what is actually hard

Hangul is sold as learnable "in an hour." True for letter recognition, false for reading. The inventory is 19 consonants (14 basic + 5 tense) and 21 vowels (10 basic + 11 compound), arranged in syllable blocks with obligatory onset and vowel plus optional coda (받침, *batchim*). Five geometries cover nearly all text: vertical vowel (가), horizontal vowel (고), vertical + coda (한), horizontal + coda (굴), and wrapped compound vowels (과, 원). Of 11,172 possible blocks, ~2,000 occur in real text and ~300 cover most running words [1][2].

The difficulty is spelling-to-sound mapping. Korean orthography is morphophonemic, preserving morphemes over surface sounds. Teach three layers separately:

1. **Neutralisation.** All 27 coda letters collapse to seven sounds: [k] ㄱㅋㄲ, [n] ㄴ, [t] ㄷㅅㅆㅈㅊㅌㅎ, [l] ㄹ, [m] ㅁ, [p] ㅂㅍ, [ŋ] ㅇ. Coda stops are **unreleased** — the key instruction for English speakers, who otherwise say "bap-uh" [3][4].
2. **Liaison (연음).** A single coda before a vowel-initial syllable resyllabifies: 한국어 → [한구거], 옷이 → [오시], 꽃을 → [꼬츨]. Liaison restores the consonant neutralisation hid, so dictionary and particle-attached forms differ [5].
3. **Assimilation/strengthening.** Nasalisation (한국말 → [한궁말]), lateralisation (신라 → [실라]), palatalisation (같이 → [가치]), aspiration (좋고 → [조코]), tensification (학교 → [학꾜]), ㅎ-deletion (좋아 → [조아]), ㄴ-insertion (학여울 → [항녀울]) [6][7]. A 2024 study found failure to apply liquid and obstruent+liquid nasalisation directly reduced intelligibility, so these are not cosmetic [8].

**Timing (design estimate).** 3–6 hours to recognise all jamo; 10–20 to decode blocks; 30–50 to read common text fluently; months before sound-change rules are automatic. Transparency supports the letter-level claim, not the one-hour reading claim [9][10].

**Sequence.** Basic consonants/vowels in CV blocks → tense/aspirated as a contrast set → coda + seven sounds → liaison → four or five high-yield assimilations → remaining rules in vocabulary. Delay double batchim to A2. Teach typing early; learners who cannot type avoid writing.

| Stage | Content | Hours | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | 14 consonants, 10 vowels, CV blocks | 2–3 | Read 60 syllables |
| H2 | Tense/aspirated, compound vowels | 2–3 | 밥/빵/팝; 과/원/의 |
| H3 | Batchim, 7 sounds, unreleased stops | 3–4 | 100 CVC words |
| H4 | Liaison + particles | 3–4 | 옷이, 꽃을, 한국어 |
| H5 | Nasalisation, lateralisation, palatalisation, aspiration, tensification | 4–6 | 학교, 한국말, 같이 |
| H6 | Double batchim, ㄴ-insertion, fluency | ongoing | 120–150 syl/min |

## 2. Phonology for English speakers

**Three-way stops.** Korean contrasts lenis (ㅂㄷㄱㅈ), aspirated (ㅍㅌㅋㅊ), and tense (ㅃㄸㄲㅉ). English has a two-way voicing contrast, so learners must re-weight cues. VOT separates tense from lenis/aspirated, while **f0 at vowel onset** is the primary lenis cue in Seoul Korean; the lenis category has shifted historically and is now cued largely by low pitch [11][12]. "Voiced vs voiceless" explanations actively mislead, and pitch must be taught as part of the consonant.

Perception and production dissociate: learners may produce an acceptable contrast while still categorising by VOT alone, and cue-weighting strategies are stable across sessions [13]. Perception training should precede or accompany production.

**Vowels.** Critical contrasts: **/ɯ/ (ㅡ)** has no English equivalent (substituted by /u/ or /ɪ/); **/ʌ/ (ㅓ)** is mid-back and often slightly rounded, not identical to English /ʌ/; **/o/ (ㅗ)** is monophthongal, unlike /oʊ/; **/e/–/ɛ/ (ㅔ–ㅐ)** are merged for most younger Seoul speakers, so teaching a distinction most natives do not make is counterproductive — teach the merger and note spelling [14]. Glide combinations (ㅟ, ㅢ, ㅘ) add load.

North Korean preserves /e/–/ɛ/ and has reported /ɯ/–/u/ and /ʌ/–/o/ mergers; South Koreans find North Korean [ʌ] and [o] highly confusable [15]. Relevant to dialect/defector content, not a Seoul curriculum.

**Clusters and rhythm.** Korean has no initial clusters and one coda consonant in pronunciation. English speakers epenthesise after released finals, merge /l/–/r/, struggle with /f v θ ð z/ in loanwords, and transfer stress-timing onto syllable-timing. ㄹ is a tap [ɾ] intervocalically and lateral [l] in coda; teach the split.

**Minimal pairs:** 불/뿔/풀, 달/딸/탈, 밥/빵, 자/짜/차, 살/쌀, 알/말.

**ASR limits.** ASR recognises L1 speech at ~90% but L2 at ~70%, while humans understand the same speech at ~95% [16]. It measures intelligibility to a model, not accuracy or accentedness. It is decent at gross substitutions that change the word, poor at unreleased-coda timing, VOT/f0 weighting, vowel quality within category, liaison, rhythm, and errors that still transcribe to the intended word. GOP scores are opaque and do not say which phone failed [17]. Use ASR for **word-level intelligibility gates and fluency timing**; use minimal-pair discrimination, waveforms, and human review for the rest. Never present an ASR score as a global pronunciation grade.

| Contrast | Korean | English approx. | Difficulty | Teach as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lenis/aspirated/tense | ㅂ/ㅍ/ㅃ | /p/, /pʰ/, tense | High | VOT + f0 + tension |
| Lenis voicing | ㅂ → [b] | /b/ | Low | Positional rule |
| Coda stops | ㅂㄷㄱ | released /p t k/ | High | Unreleased, no vowel |
| /ɯ/ | ㅡ | none | High | Back, unrounded |
| /ʌ/ | ㅓ | /ʌ/ | Medium | Rounded, short |
| /o/ | ㅗ | /oʊ/ | Medium | Monophthong |
| /e/–/ɛ/ | ㅔ–ㅐ | /e/–/æ/ | Low | Merger |
| /l/–/r/ | ㄹ | /l/–/ɹ/ | Medium | Tap vs lateral |
| Sibilants | ㅅㅆㅈㅉㅊ | /s z tʃ dʒ/ | Medium | Place + tension |

## 3. Morphosyntax

Korean is SOV, agglutinative, head-final, and topic-prominent. The difficulty is that these co-occur and are obligatory.

**Particles.** 은/는 (topic/contrast), 이/가 (subject), 을/를 (object), 에 (time, destination, existence), 에서 (action location, source), 도 (also), 만 (only), 에게/한테/께 (dative), (으)로 (direction/means), 와/과/하고 (and/with), 의 (possessive), 부터/까지 (from/until). Nine particles cover ~70% of tokens; 32 cover ~95% [18]. 은/는 vs 이/가 is discourse-pragmatic: topic/contrast vs new/identificational subject. Teaching it as "topic vs subject" without discourse produces overgeneralised 은/는.

**Verb-final and embedding.** The predicate is final; modifiers precede nouns; relative clauses use -(으)ㄴ/는/(으)ㄹ + noun; complements use -다고/-(으)라고/냐고. English speakers must plan backward, holding arguments until the verb supplies role and tense.

**Speech levels.** Six are counted; four are alive: 합니다체 (formal polite), 해요체 (informal polite), 해라체/한다체 (plain, written/reported), 해체/반말 (casual). 하오체/하게체 survive in period drama [19][20]. Default to 해요체; 합니다체 for public/professional/first-contact; 반말 only after relational licence. 합니다체 is not "more polite" on one ladder — the difference is formality/distance, and speakers mix them within one exchange [21].

**Honorifics.** Subject honorific -시- (가시다, 하시다, 드시다) plus suppletion: 먹다→드시다, 자다→주무시다, 있다→계시다, 말하다→말씀하시다, 집→댁, 나이→연세, 이름→성함, 사람→분. Humble forms lower the speaker: 드리다, 뵙다, 여쭙다. English has no equivalent, so learners either avoid honorifics (blunt) or apply -시- to themselves (salient error). Teach **two axes**: addressee level and subject status, with workplace/family scenarios.

**Pronouns and address.** Korean prefers omission and role terms. 저/나, 우리 (often "my" — 우리 엄마), 너 (intimate), 당신 (marked; avoid), 그/그녀 (written). Address terms carry the social information: 이름+씨, 선생님, 사장님, 과장님, 오빠/언니/형/누나, 아저씨/아주머니, plus age. Teaching "you = 당신" is a serious pragmatic error.

**Topic-prominence.** Subjects/objects drop when recoverable. English speakers over-insert pronouns ("저는 저는..."), grammatical but discourse-poor. Train zero-anaphora from A2: who is the omitted subject, and what does the particle signal?

## 4. The "endings problem"

Conjugation is stem + **pre-final** + **final** endings, with honorific, tense, aspect, and mood slots. 가다 surfaces as 가, 가요, 갑니다, 갔어요, 갔습니다, 가겠어요, 가고 있어요, 가시겠어요, 가셨겠어요. The space is combinatorial; "learn 100 endings" fails without the **slot architecture**.

| Irregular | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ㅂ | ㅂ→우 before vowel; 오 in 돕다/곱다 | 춥다→추워요; 돕다→도와요 |
| ㄷ | ㄷ→ㄹ before vowel | 듣다→들어요 |
| ㅅ | ㅅ deleted before vowel | 짓다→지어요 |
| 르 | 르→ㄹㄹ | 모르다→몰라요 |
| ㅎ | ㅎ deleted/contracted | 좋다→좋아요 [조아요] |
| ㅜ/ㅡ | vowel contraction | 크다→커요 |
| ㄹ | ㄹ deleted before ㄴ/ㅂ/오 | 살다→사는; 멀다→먼 |

**Tense/aspect.** -았/었- past; -고 있다 progressive; -아/어 있다 resultant state; -겠- intention/conjecture (not future); -(으)ㄹ 거예요 prospective; -았/었었- discontinuous past; -더- retrospective evidential (B1+). English speakers map tense onto aspect, overusing -고 있다 and underusing -아/어 있다.

**Connectors.** -고, -아서/어서 (cause/sequence; no imperative), -(으)니까 (reason; imperative OK), -지만, -는데, -(으)면서, -자마자, -다가, -기 때문에. The constraints — e.g. -아서 cannot precede a command — are where learners fossilise.

**Modality.** -아/어야 하다/되다, -(으)면 되다, -아/어도 되다, -(으)면 안 되다, -지 말다, -(으)ㄹ 수 있다/없다, -고 싶다, -(으)ㄹ래요, -(으)ㄹ게요, -(으)ㄹ까요?, -(으)ㄴ/는 것 같다, -나 보다, -네요/-군요.

**Sequencing.** Teach one ending with its constraints and discourse function, then contrast it with its nearest neighbour (은/는 vs 이/가; -아서 vs -니까; -겠- vs -(으)ㄹ 거예요). Spiral back every 3–4 units. Do not front-load full conjugation tables; teach 3–4 high-frequency forms per verb and let the paradigm fill in.

## 5. Vocabulary and coverage

Three strata: native (~35%), Sino-Korean (~57% by NIKL's dictionary; estimates 30–70% by register), loanwords (~5%) [22][23]. Sino-Korean dominates academic/formal text (up to ~80%) and is only ~10% of basic vocabulary. This is an accelerator: one hanja morpheme (학 學, 전 電, 문 文, 비 非) recurs across dozens of words. Teaching 100–150 high-yield morphemes at B1 beats 1,000 unrelated words. Hanja characters are optional for literacy; the morphemes are not.

**Numbers.** Sino-Korean for dates, money, phone, minutes, addresses, large numbers; native for counters, hours, age, small quantities. Counters: 개, 명/분, 마리, 잔, 병, 권, 대, 살, 시. Teach the split as a selection rule.

**Frequency.** NIKL's Basic Korean Vocabulary List has 5,897 entries; the Routledge frequency dictionary covers the top 5,000 from the 10M-word Sejong corpus; KoFREN provides 41M-word spontaneous-speech norms [24][25][26]. Coverage estimates vary, but the shape is consistent: ~1,000 lemmas ≈ 75–80% of everyday spoken tokens; ~2,000 ≈ 88–92%; ~3,000 ≈ 93–95%; ~5,000 ≈ 96–98%. Reading thresholds are conventionally 95% (minimal) and 98% (comfortable) [27]. Do not teach 5,000 words before reading: build to ~2,000 for A2/B1 with scaffolding, then use graded readers at i-1.

## 6. Pragmatics and culture

Politeness is relational, recomputed per interlocutor from age, status, intimacy, and setting. Default to 해요체 with unfamiliar adults; round **up** in politeness. Age determines address term, speech level, and morphology, and "how old are you?" is a normal early question.

**Workplace** adds titles, humble verbs, indirect commands, and a written register. **Texting** is a separate register: consonant abbreviations (ㅋㅋㅋ, ㅎㅎ, ㅇㅇ, ㄱㅅ, ㅇㅋ, ㄹㅇ, ㅠㅠ), number codes (8282 = 빨리빨리, 1004 = angel), 카톡 as a verb, 읽씹 (read and ignored), and punctuation pragmatics (a bare 네. reads cold; 넵! is workplace-friendly) [28][29]. Teach at B1 as register awareness, not formal production.

**Dialects.** Standard is Gyeonggi/Seoul. Gyeongsang has pitch accent and endings (-노, -나, 하이소) and is common in dramas; Jeolla has a different vowel system and endings (-당게, -거라); Chungcheong is slower with -유; Jeju is a separate Koreanic language, not mutually intelligible, UNESCO critically endangered [30][31][32]. North Korean *munhwaŏ* remains broadly intelligible but has purged loanwords and shifted vocabulary and vowels [15][33]. Teach standard first; expose dialect at B1+ as listening; do not make it a production target.

## 7. Frameworks and realistic time

TOPIK I (1–2): Listening 30/40 min, Reading 40/60, max 200; cutoffs 80, 140. TOPIK II (3–6): Listening 50/60, Writing 4/50, Reading 50/70, max 300; cutoffs 120, 150, 190, 230 [34][35]. The 2025 IBT has different counts and a 400-point scale; check the session format [36].

| Framework | Level | Vocabulary | Can-do |
|---|---|---|---|
| TOPIK I | 1 | ~800 | Greetings, self-intro, shopping, ordering; basic particles, present/past |
| TOPIK I | 2 | 1,500–2,000 | Phone, transport, facilities; formal/informal switching; connectors |
| TOPIK II | 3 | 3,000–4,000 | Most daily situations; simple news; paragraph discourse |
| TOPIK II | 4 | 4,000–5,000 | News, social topics, abstract discussion, work tasks |
| TOPIK II | 5 | 5,000–6,000 | Professional settings, specialised texts, complex discussion |
| TOPIK II | 6 | 6,000+ | Near-native academic/professional; idiom and register |
| CEFR | A1–C2 | — | Approx. mapping: 1→A1, 2→A2, 3→B1, 4→B2, 5→C1, 6→C2 (not official) |
| Sejong | 1–6 | — | 1–2 A1–A2, 3–4 B1–B2, 5–6 C1–C2 |

Sejong is six levels of 20 lessons (40 per two-level band) plus a 12-lesson introduction; the beginner band covers Hangul, numbers, basic particles, past tense, and ㄷ/ㅂ irregulars [37][38].

**Hours.** FSI puts Korean in Category IV: ~2,200 classroom hours, 88 weeks, to ILR 3 (roughly B2/C1) [39][40]. Self-study multipliers are 1.5–2×. Modeled CEFR milestones are A1 ~240h, A2 ~530h, B1 ~1,000h, B2 ~1,550h, C1 ~2,200h [41] — heuristics, not guarantees. A defensible product promise for a motivated daily learner: A1 in 60–80 guided hours, A2 in 150–200, B1 in 350–450. That is faster than the FSI-derived curve because guided apps compress input and feedback, but not so fast that retention is plausible below those numbers.

## 8. Errors, fossilisation, feedback

Particle errors are the largest grammatical category — ~28% of beginner errors — with omission (~48%) and substitution (~45%) dominating [18][42]. English speakers omit particles because English marks roles by position and substitute 은/는 for 이/가 because both translate as "subject." Other families:

- **Pronunciation:** released codas + epenthesis; /l/–/r/ merger; missed liaison/nasalisation; stress-timing; stop confusion.
- **Morphology:** irregular overregularisation (듣어요 for 들어요); tense-aspect mapping; -시- on self.
- **Syntax:** SVO in embedded clauses; pronoun retention; relative-clause attachment; 그리고 as universal connector.
- **Pragmatics:** 반말 to strangers; 당신; direct refusals; ignoring age/status.
- **Lexical:** literal translation, synonym confusion, Sino/native register mismatch.

Fossilisation is selective, not global: specific subsystems stabilise while others develop [43]. The intermediate plateau usually comes from comprehension-heavy input without pushed output. Feedback research is design-ready: corrective feedback has significant durable effects; **prompts** (elicitation, clarification, metalinguistic cues) outperform **recasts**; explicit feedback is stronger short-term, implicit can be stronger long-term [44][45][46]. Prefer elicitation ("다시 말해 볼까요?") over silent recasts; make the form salient; require repair; delay the answer enough for retrieval.

## 9. Curriculum blueprint: 0 → B1 → TOPIK 6

| Phase | Hours | CEFR/TOPIK | Grammar core | Vocabulary | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 Hangul | 8–12 | pre-A1 | Jamo, blocks, batchim, liaison | 50 | Read signs; self-intro |
| P1 Foundations | 40–60 | A1.1 / 1 | 은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 에/에서, 도/만, 이에요/입니다, 있어요/없어요, present, past, 안/못 | 300–500 | Order, shop, introduce, ask location |
| P2 Interaction | 80–120 | A1.2–A2 / 2 | ㅂ/ㄷ/ㅅ/르/ㅎ irregulars, -(으)ㄹ 거예요, -고 싶다, -아서/어서, -지만, -는데, -시-, counters, comparatives | 800–1,500 | Phone, transport, appointments, routine |
| P3 Intermediate | 150–250 | B1 / 3 | -고 있다, -겠-, -(으)ㄴ/는 것, relative clauses, -다고, -기 때문에, -(으)면서, -자마자, passive/causative intro | 2,000–3,000 | Narrate, explain, opine, solve problems |
| P4 Upper-int. | 300–450 | B2 / 4 | Reported speech, -더-, -나 보다, -기는 하다, -을 수밖에, idioms, written registers | 3,500–4,500 | Discuss news/society, meetings, structured writing |
| P5 Advanced | 500–800 | C1 / 5 | Rhetorical endings, hedging, compression, genre registers | 5,000–6,000 | Debate, present, read editorials |
| P6 Mastery | 800–1,200 | C2 / 6 | Stylistic nuance, dialect, literary forms, academic writing | 6,000–8,000+ | Near-native professional/academic |

**First 7 days.** D1: 14 consonants + 6 vowels, 30 CV blocks, 안녕하세요/감사합니다/네/아니요. D2: remaining vowels, 40 blocks, 저는 ___이에요. D3: batchim + seven sounds, 20 CVC words, 뭐예요? D4: liaison, 은/는 and 이/가 in self-intro. D5: 있어요/없어요, 이/그/저, Sino numbers 1–10. D6: 해요체 present with 10 verbs, 안 + verb. D7: review, 2-minute recorded self-intro, typing drill, 40-word dialogue. Target: 50 words, 8 patterns, full jamo accuracy.

**First 30 days.** Weeks 2–4: 을/를, 에/에서, 도/만, past -았/었-, native numbers + counters, shopping/café scenarios, 150–200 words, 15-minute spaced review daily, first 3-minute unscripted conversation. Add contrast drills (lenis/aspirated/tense, /ɯ/, coda release) three times weekly.

**First 90 days.** Months 2–3: irregulars, -(으)ㄹ 거예요, -고 싶다, -아서/어서, -지만, -는데, -고, -시- and lexical honorifics, comparatives, -(으)ㄹ까요?/-ㄹ래요. Vocabulary 500–700. Graded reading at 95% coverage; weekly 10-minute tutoring or ASR-gated speaking; 반말 comprehension (not production). By day 90: A1.2, 8–10 transactional scenarios, 5-minute daily-life conversation.

**B1 → TOPIK 6.** B1→B2 (6–9 months): news listening, relative clauses, reported speech, passive/causative, 3,000→4,500 words, weekly 300-word writing with focused feedback. B2→C1 (9–15 months): editorials, debate, workplace honorifics, hanja expansion, idiom banks, timed TOPIK II reading. C1→6 (6–12 months): writing templates for the 200–300 and 600–700 character tasks, error logs, mock exams, dialect and rapid-speech listening. Throughput: 8,000+ words, 400+ patterns, 100+ speaking hours.

## 10. Resources and what to emulate

| Resource | Pedagogy | Emulate | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TTMIK** | 10 sequenced levels, 1,700+ short lessons; warm-up → why → speaking → review; story review; IYAGI natural listening | Bite-sized sequencing, explicit rationale, mandatory production, review loops | Slow grammar density at upper levels; romanisation |
| **Go! Billy** | English-native explanations; Hangul and politeness-level courses; worked examples | Contrastive explanation; politeness as a system | Video pacing; limited SRS |
| **LingoDeer** | Structured Asian-language path, grammar tips, native audio, alphabet-first | Grammar at point of need; clean progression | Translation-heavy drills; weak spontaneity |
| **Teuida** | Speaking-first POV dialogues; ASR check; situational immersion | Forced output from day 1; realistic scenarios | ASR false negatives; score opacity |
| **Mirinae** | Sentence parser: particles, honorifics, conjugations, parse tree; Chrome extension over subtitles | Deep morphosyntactic explanation; authentic-text annotation | Over-analysis can stall fluency |
| **Naver / Papago** | Examples, hanja, idioms, pronunciation; NMT with register options | Rich examples; etymology; quick lookup | MT as a crutch; literal translation |
| **Graded readers** | Parallel text, audio, level control, translation as check | Controlled vocabulary, audio+text, i-1 difficulty | Passive reading without retrieval |
| **Dramas + dual subs** | Authentic input, intonation, pragmatics, dialect | Chunking, shadowing, scene-based pragmatics | Subtitle reading replacing listening |

**Synthesis.** Best-in-class products combine what most apps separate: explicit contrastive phonology; sequenced, constraint-aware grammar with spiral review; pushed output with prompt-style feedback; authentic input scaffolded to 95% coverage. TTMIK supplies sequencing and tone, Go Billy contrastive explanation, Teuida output pressure, Mirinae parse-level transparency, graded readers the extensive-reading engine. A product that does only one produces the familiar profile: learners who decode but cannot speak, or speak fluently with fossilised particles.
